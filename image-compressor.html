What the Image Compressor does

Most compression problems are really size-limit problems: a portal accepts 100 KB photos, an email gateway caps attachments at 10 MB, or a page loads slowly because a 4 MB photo is being served at full resolution in a 400-pixel slot.

This compressor re-encodes your image in the browser at the quality and maximum width you choose, showing the original size, the new size and the reduction as you adjust. Because it runs locally, you can compress an ID document or a family photo without it being uploaded anywhere.

How to use it

  1. Select one or more images, or drop them on the drop zone.
  2. Set the quality — 0.8 is a good starting point for photos, 0.9 for text in images.
  3. Set a maximum width if the image is being scaled down for a website.
  4. Press Compress, compare the sizes, then download individually or all at once.

Features

  • Adjustable JPEG and WebP quality
  • Optional maximum width for downscaling
  • Before and after size for every file
  • Batch processing in one pass
  • No upload, so sensitive images stay on your device

Worked examples

A 4 MB phone photo

A 4032-pixel-wide photo re-encoded to 1600 pixels wide at quality 0.8 typically lands between 250 KB and 450 KB — around a 90% reduction, with no visible difference on a phone screen.

Meeting a 100 KB limit

If quality 0.8 still returns 180 KB, reduce the maximum width to 1000 pixels before lowering quality further. Shrinking dimensions usually costs less visible detail than heavy JPEG artefacts.

Frequently asked questions

Does compressing an image reduce quality?

JPEG and WebP compression is lossy, so some data is discarded. At quality 0.8 and a sensible resolution the difference is usually invisible on screen; the damage becomes visible in fine detail, gradients and text at low quality settings. PNG remains lossless, so re-encoding a PNG as PNG mainly helps through downscaling.

What size should I compress to?

For a website hero image, 1600 pixels wide is plenty. For an inline article image, 1000 to 1200 pixels. For a form that demands under 100 KB, reduce width first and quality second. Print needs far more: 300 DPI at the physical size.

Does this remove EXIF metadata?

Re-encoding through a canvas drops most metadata, including GPS location. That is usually desirable for images posted publicly, but it also removes orientation data, so images taken in portrait on a phone should be checked after compression.

Can I compress HEIC photos from an iPhone?

Not directly in most browsers, because Safari and Chrome do not decode HEIC consistently. Export the photo as JPEG from your phone's share sheet first, then compress it here.
