What the Discount Calculator does

A discount is easy to calculate and surprisingly easy to misread. 30% off 80 is 56, but two discounts of 20% and 10% are not 30% off — they are 28% off, because the second discount applies to the already reduced price. Stacked coupons, member discounts and sale-on-sale pricing all behave this way.

This calculator works out the sale price from a percentage or a fixed amount, tells you the effective discount when you stack two reductions, and can add sales tax afterwards so you know what actually leaves your account. It also reverses the sum, so from a sale price you can recover what the item originally cost.

How to use it

  1. Enter the original price.
  2. Enter the discount as a percentage, or switch to a fixed amount off.
  3. Add a second discount if coupons stack, and a tax rate if tax is added at checkout.
  4. Read the final price, the total saving and the effective percentage off.

Features

  • Percentage or fixed-amount discounts
  • Stacked discounts with the true effective percentage
  • Optional sales tax or VAT added after the discount
  • Reverse mode to find the original price from a sale price
  • Shows money saved in the item currency

Worked examples

30% off 80

80 × 0.70 = 56. You save 24, which is 30% of the original price. Add 20% VAT to 56 and the amount you actually pay is 67.20.

Two stacked discounts

20% off then 10% off 100: 100 → 80 → 72. The saving is 28, not 30, so the effective discount is 28%. Stacking three 10% discounts gives 27.1% off, never 30%.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate the original price from a sale price?

Divide the sale price by 1 minus the discount rate. A 60 item at 25% off came from 60 ÷ 0.75 = 80. Adding 25% to 60 instead gives 75, which is wrong — that is the classic reverse-percentage error.

Are two 20% discounts the same as 40% off?

No. Two 20% discounts compound to 36% off, because the second applies to the already discounted price. 100 becomes 80, then 64.

Is a discount applied before or after tax?

In most jurisdictions a trade discount reduces the taxable amount, so tax is calculated on the discounted price. Store coupons usually behave the same way, while manufacturer rebates are often applied after tax has been charged.

What is the difference between a discount and a markup?

A discount reduces a selling price from above; a markup increases a cost price to reach a selling price. A 50% discount requires a 100% markup to return to the original price, which is why heavy discounting is so hard to recover from.
